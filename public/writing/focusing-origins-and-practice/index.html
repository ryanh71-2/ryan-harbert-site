Focusing: Where It Came From and How It Works

By Ryan Harbert

Focusing grew out of research into a question that still matters: why does psychotherapy help some people change, while others remain stuck?

In the 1950s, Eugene Gendlin, a philosopher at the University of Chicago, began working with psychologist Carl Rogers. Rogers’s approach emphasized empathy and the relationship between therapist and client. Gendlin wanted to understand what people were doing inwardly when therapy helped them.

The research pointed to something important. People who made progress often paused while speaking and attended to something they could feel but couldn’t yet explain. They were finding words by checking with their experience.

Gendlin recognized that this was a skill people could learn. He developed ways of teaching it and, in 1978, published his book Focusing.[1]

The name can be confusing. It doesn’t mean concentrating harder. It means learning how to attend to something that is felt but not yet clear—and giving it room to develop.

Knowing about a problem and working with it

We can know a great deal about our problems without being able to change them.

We know when they started. We know what triggers them. We may have good explanations for why we get anxious, withdraw from people, or return to something that hurts us. Yet the pattern continues.

Focusing begins with a pause in all that explaining. We give ourselves time to sense how the difficulty is actually living in us now.

Someone might say:

“I’m angry about that conversation.”

Then, after a pause:

“No, angry isn’t quite it. There’s something else about the way it ended.”

That is an imagined example, but it shows the kind of attention involved. The person is letting what they feel correct what they say.

Gendlin described how even familiar words can have a new effect when they come from this kind of listening. Someone may already know they are scared. But when they pause and really sense that fear, the words can bring a fuller recognition and a bodily easing.[2]

This is part of what makes Focusing different from going over the same thoughts again.

The felt sense

Gendlin called the bodily sense of a whole situation a felt sense.

Think of someone you know well. You don’t have to remember every conversation to have a sense of what it is like to be with them. There is a whole feel to the relationship. Much more is there than you could say in a sentence.

Gendlin used this kind of example to describe how we can feel many things together before sorting them into separate thoughts.[3]

A felt sense can include emotions, but it is more than an emotion. “Angry” or “sad” may name something familiar. The felt sense includes what is particular about this anger, this sadness, this situation.

It is also more than a physical sensation on its own. A tightness in your stomach might be where you begin. As you attend to it in relation to what is happening in your life, a fuller sense may form.

You can feel that there is something to understand, even when you don’t yet know what it is.

What this can look like

Here is an imagined example.

You leave a conversation with a friend feeling uneasy. You go over what was said and tell yourself it was nothing. But the uneasiness stays.

You pause. There is a tight, held feeling in your chest when you think about the conversation. You give yourself a little time to sense the whole of it.

“Angry” doesn’t quite fit.

“Hurt” is closer, but still not quite right.

Then something comes: “I agreed before I knew what I wanted.”

You feel a little relief. Those words touch something you hadn’t recognized.

As you stay with it, more becomes clear: “I’m afraid they’ll be disappointed if I change my mind.”

Now you have something you can work with. You can sense your own wishes, and you can feel what makes it hard to speak up.

You still have a decision to make. But you are no longer working only with a vague discomfort or telling yourself to get over it. You have a clearer relationship with what is happening.

This is one possible way the process could unfold. It won’t always come in tidy steps.

Learning the inner moves

Gendlin developed six steps to help people learn Focusing. Different ways of teaching have developed since then, and the practice doesn’t have to follow a fixed sequence.[1]

You learn to make enough room to notice what is there. You let a felt sense form. You try a word or an image and sense whether it fits. When something changes, you take time to feel what is different.

Describing these movements is fairly easy. Learning to recognize them in yourself takes practice.

You may discover how quickly you reach for an explanation. Or how hard it is to wait when nothing is clear. You may notice that you’re trying to make the feeling give you the answer you want.

All of that can become part of the learning.

One of the skills is being able to stay at that uncomfortable place where you don’t yet know. You haven’t found the next step, and you can’t be sure what will come.

This is what I mean by waiting for “the more.”

How we meet what we find

The quality of our attention matters.

If I pay attention to something only to get rid of it, that demand is part of how I’m meeting it. Focusing asks me to give it some room before deciding what it should become.

Psychologist Mia Leijssen describes an attitude of staying patiently and receptively with what cannot yet be expressed. She also emphasizes finding the right distance: close enough to be in contact, with enough room to avoid being overwhelmed.[4]

You don’t have to force yourself deeper into pain. You are learning how to be with it in a way that makes further exploration possible.

And you don’t have to stop wanting change. Of course you want relief. You may have been suffering for years.

The practice is to let the next step come from attending to what is there, rather than insisting that it follow your plan.

That also means checking what comes. A bodily feeling is worth listening to, but it isn’t automatically the truth about another person. Feeling uneasy after a conversation gives you something to explore. It doesn’t, by itself, tell you what your friend intended.

When something begins to move

Focusers often call a change in the felt sense a felt shift. There may be relief, a fresh understanding, or a sense of a next step. Gendlin emphasized that the important thing is the felt difference, rather than how impressive the words sound.[2]

In the example, “I agreed before I knew what I wanted” is a fairly ordinary sentence. But for that person, in that moment, it changes something.

I think of this as finding room around a knot. We may have been pulling on the same threads for years. When we can sense the whole thing differently, another movement becomes possible.

Sometimes that movement is small. Sometimes it reaches something that has been stuck for a long time.

This is part of Focusing’s capacity for healing. We gain understanding, and something in how we carry the difficulty can change.

There may be more work to do. But we have a way to continue.

Why it helps to learn with someone

It can be hard to discover these inner moves from a description alone.

Another person can help you slow down, notice what you passed over, or stay with something you haven’t yet found words for. They can listen without needing you to make sense immediately.

Leijssen describes how being met with acceptance and empathy can help a person develop that same friendly, listening attitude toward their own experience.[4]

This is something we learn through the relationship as well as through instructions.

In my sessions, I may offer a suggestion or reflect what I hear. You get to sense whether it fits. If it doesn’t, that matters. We follow what you are finding, including when it takes us somewhere neither of us expected.

More than a way to work with problems

Focusing also has a place in creative work, thinking, and decisions. We can attend to something that matters before we have clear words for it and allow new meaning to develop.[5]

This connects with Gendlin’s broader work, the Philosophy of the Implicit. Our experience contains more than our existing descriptions can say. Finding words can help that experience develop further. He called this carrying forward.

Gendlin gave the example of talking with someone after a play. Sometimes the discussion leaves what you experienced feeling flattened. At other times, what each person says helps the other find more. The conversation brings the experience further to life.[6]

You don’t need to study the philosophy to learn Focusing. But it helps explain why the practice reaches beyond solving problems. It is also a way of discovering and developing what we haven’t yet been able to express.

Where to begin

You don’t need to arrive with a clear felt sense. You don’t need to produce an insight.

You can begin with something that is bothering you, a vague feeling, or uncertainty about what you notice.

My part is to help you find a way to listen. We slow down, sense what fits, and give what is unclear enough room to become clearer.

That is already the beginning of the practice.
